\section{The source and the site}
\label{sec:source}

William was a monk of Malmesbury writing about a century after the flight, and he could
have heard the story from men who knew Eilmer \autocite{white1961eilmer,thomson2004eilmer_odnb}.
His Latin and a standard translation are given in Supplementary S1.
He says that Eilmer ``collected the breeze on the summit of a tower'', that the violence of
the wind and the swirling of the air brought him down, and that he broke both legs.
The date is uncertain: the traditional estimate is about 1000--1010, and
\textcite{aitcheson2026date} argues it could be as late as the early 1040s.
The analysis below samples launch weather from a modern climatology, so it does not depend
on the year.

Earlier and later accounts of human flight attempts are mostly legend
\autocite{paz2013humanflight}.
The one other early account with a stated device is that of Abbas ibn Firnas in
ninth-century Córdoba, preserved in later Andalusian chronicles, which describes a
structure of feathers and silk and a bad landing also blamed on the lack of a tail
\autocite{white1961eilmer,anderson2024bridge}; \textcite{hallion2003takingflight} reads
it as closer to a parachute descent.
Neither source gives dimensions, materials beyond these, or how the wings were attached.
There is therefore no historical basis for a specific design, and the models here sample
wing area, mass, wing shape, and drag over wide ranges instead (Supplementary
\cref{tab:priors}).

The abbey stands on a narrow spur between two branches of the Bristol Avon
(\cref{fig:photo}).
The present church is not Eilmer's, and his tower's position and height are unknown.
The analysis therefore launches from the abbey site and samples the tower height between
\SI{15}{\meter} and \SI{25}{\meter}, around White's estimate of about \SI{18}{\meter}
\autocite{white1961eilmer}.
\textcite{woosnam1986eilmer} argues that the flight went down the steep slope southwest of
the abbey towards the river, which is the side shown in \cref{fig:photo}.
