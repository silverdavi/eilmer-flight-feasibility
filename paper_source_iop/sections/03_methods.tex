\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Terrain}

The terrain is a digital elevation model (DEM) of the abbey spur assembled from the
Amazon Web Services (AWS) Terrain Tiles at zoom level 14, a grid spacing of about
\SI{6}{\meter}.
Slope and aspect (the compass direction a slope faces) are computed within
\SI{\nSlopeRadius}{\meter} of the abbey, and an elevation section is taken along the
southwest--northeast axis.
A \SI{6}{\meter} grid smooths the steep edge of the spur, so the computed slopes are lower
bounds; airborne laser scanning (LIDAR) would resolve it.

\subsection{Wind climatology}

Hourly wind at \SI{10}{\meter} height at the abbey for 2004--2023 (\nHours\ hours) is taken
from the ERA5 reanalysis of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts
\autocite{hersbach2020era5}, through the Open-Meteo archive \autocite{openmeteo_archive}.
An hour counts as favourable for launch when the wind blows from the southwest sector
(\ang{191}--\ang{259}, centred on the slope aspect) at \SIrange{5}{12}{\meter\per\second}.
Fractions are reported for the year and for each season, with \SI{95}{\percent} confidence
intervals (CI) from the spread across the twenty years.
Modern data stand in for the eleventh century.
Reconstructions of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), the pressure pattern that sets the
strength of the westerlies over Britain, disagree on its phase during the Medieval Climate
Anomaly, but none indicates a long negative phase with weak westerlies
\autocite{trouet2009nao,ortega2015nao}.

\subsection{Ridge lift}

Wind blowing up a slope is deflected upwards.
To first order the vertical air velocity follows the surface gradient and decays with
height,
\begin{equation}
  w(x,z) = U\,\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}x}\,e^{-kz},
  \label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
where \(U\) is the wind speed, \(h(x)\) the terrain height along the wind, \(z\) the height
above the surface, and \(k = 2\pi/L\) with \(L\) the horizontal scale of the slope.
\Cref{eq:ridge} is applied to the measured section (Supplementary \cref{fig:ridge}).
The glide model uses the simpler surface estimate \(w = \eta\,U\sin\theta_s\), with
\(\theta_s\) the slope angle and \(\eta \le 1\) an efficiency that allows for flow that
does not follow the surface.
The lift acts only over the slope: for \SI{\nLiftIntoWind}{\meter} on the track that
heads into the wind, where the section flattens onto the valley floor, and for
\SI{\nLiftAlongSlope}{\meter} on the track that follows the slope, the longest continuous
stretch of southwest-facing ground steeper than \ang{5} (\cref{fig:site}b).

\subsection{Glide performance}

The glide ratio \LD\ of an unknown wing is estimated from the parabolic drag polar
\(C_D = C_{D0} + C_L^2/(\pi e A)\), whose maximum is
\begin{equation}
  (\LD)_\mathrm{max} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi e A}{C_{D0}}},
  \label{eq:polar}
\end{equation}
where \(A\) is the aspect ratio (span squared over area), \(e\) the span efficiency, and
\(C_{D0}\) the zero-lift drag coefficient \autocite{drela2014flight}.
Each factor can be bounded for a cloth wing carrying a prone man.
The aspect ratio is taken as \numrange{\nARlo}{\nARhi}, a span of about one to two arm
spans for areas of \SIrange{5}{10}{\meter\squared}.
Span efficiencies of \numrange{\nElo}{\nEhi} cover untwisted, non-elliptical wings
\autocite{drela2014flight}.
The zero-lift drag \(C_{D0} = \numrange{\nCDzlo}{\nCDzhi}\) combines the profile drag of a
cambered membrane at a Reynolds number near \num{e6}
\autocite{lissaman1983lowre,hoerner1985lift} with the drag of the pilot's body, frame,
and bracing \autocite{hoerner1965drag}.
These ranges give \((\LD)_\mathrm{max} = \numrange{\nLDlo}{\nLDhi}\), with \num{\nLDnom}
at their centres.
The glide calculation flies every wing at its best glide ratio, which favours the
account.

The trim airspeed of a wing of area \(S\) and mass \(m\) at lift coefficient \(C_L\) is
\(V = \sqrt{2mg/(\rho S C_L)}\), which is \SI{\nTrimSpeed}{\meter\per\second} for the nominal
configuration of \cref{fig:apparatus} (\SI{\nMass}{\kilogram}, \SI{\nArea}{\meter\squared},
\(C_L = \nCLtrim\)).
\Cref{fig:apparatus} shows the centre of the sampled ranges and is not a reconstruction;
the only historical detail it carries is the wing fixed to the hands and feet, with no
tail.

\subsection{Launch and glide trajectory}

A man stepping off a tower does not start at flying speed.
He leaves at about \SI{\nJump}{\meter\per\second} relative to the ground and must dive until
the airspeed reaches \(V\).
The flight is therefore integrated in time as a point mass held at constant \(C_L\),
\begin{equation}
  \dot V = g\sin\gamma - \frac{D}{m}, \qquad
  \dot\gamma = \frac{1}{V}\left(g\cos\gamma - \frac{L}{m}\right), \qquad
  \dot z = -V\sin\gamma + w,
  \label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
where \(V\) is now the airspeed, \(\gamma\) the descent angle below the horizontal,
\(L = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S C_L\), and \(D = L/(\LD)\).
The airspeed at release is the jump speed plus the headwind.
In still air the craft dives, gains speed, and pulls out in a damped oscillation, the
phugoid.

The wind also changes the ground covered, and two tracks bracket the possibilities.
On the first, the pilot heads southwest into the wind, down the slope towards the river
\autocite{woosnam1986eilmer}; his ground speed is \(V\cos\gamma - U\), and the ground is the
measured section.
On the second, he flies northwest along the slope with the wind from the side and crabs,
heading partly into the wind so that it does not drift him off the track; his ground speed
is
\(\sqrt{(V\cos\gamma)^2 - U^2}\), and the ground is taken as a contour
\SI{\nContour}{\meter} below the abbey, half-way down the slope.
The second is how hang-glider and sailplane pilots use ridge lift \autocite{faa_glider}.
William's ``more than a furlong'' is tested as a ground range of at least
\SI{200}{\meter}.

\subsection{Pitch stability}

A wing holds its pitch attitude on its own if the centre of gravity (CG) lies ahead of the
neutral point, the point about which the pitching moment does not change with angle of
attack.
Both are written as fractions of the mean chord \(c\) from the leading edge, \(\cgfrac\) and
\(\npfrac\).
A cambered wing without a tail has \(\npfrac \approx \nNPnotail\); a tail moves it aft, to
\num{\nNPtail} for the nominal tail, and adds pitch damping \autocite{drela2014flight}.

The pilot's CG is set by his body.
With the wing fixed to his hands at the leading edge and his feet at the trailing edge,
his mass centre lies between them.
Standing, a man's mass centre is at about \SI{55}{\percent} of his height above the soles
\autocite{winter2009biomechanics}, and his reach from soles to hands raised overhead is
about \num{1.25} times his height.
Laid out from hands to feet, the mass centre therefore sits at about \num{0.55} of that
distance behind the hands.
The sampled range \(\cgfrac = \numrange{\nCGlo}{\nCGhi}\) runs from this straight posture
forward to a pilot who draws his hips up under the leading edge.
The pitch analysis is also run across \(\cgfrac = \numrange{0.20}{0.60}\), to show where
the conclusion would change.

The short-period pitch motion is linearised in angle of attack and pitch rate with
standard stability derivatives \autocite{drela2014flight}.
When the CG is aft of the neutral point, the state matrix has a positive real eigenvalue
\(\lambda\), and a disturbance doubles in \(t_2 = \ln 2/\lambda\).
\(t_2\) is compared with the duration of the flight and with the effective time delay of a
human operator in a manual control task, about \SIrange{\nReactLo}{\nReactHi}{\second}
\autocite{mcruer1967pilot}.
The nonlinear response to an \ang{8} gust is also integrated, with control taken as lost
beyond a pitch attitude of \ang{60}.

\subsection{Monte Carlo sample}

The apparatus and the day are unknown, so \nSamples\ cases are drawn from uniform ranges
(Supplementary \cref{tab:priors}) with a fixed random seed.
The draws are wing area, mass, trim \(C_L\), aspect ratio, span efficiency, zero-lift drag,
tower height, slope angle, lift efficiency \(\eta\), CG, and the neutral-point shift from a
tail.
The glide ratio follows from \cref{eq:polar} and is not drawn directly.
The launch wind is drawn from the measured ERA5 southwest-sector hours at
\SIrange{3}{16}{\meter\per\second}, on the assumption that Eilmer chose a windy day.
Each case is flown on both tracks with \cref{eq:eom} and scored on whether it covers
\SI{200}{\meter}, and whether \(t_2\) is shorter than the flight, with and without a tail.
Fractions carry Wilson score \SI{95}{\percent} confidence intervals.

\subsection{Impact}

William records that Eilmer broke both legs and survived.
In a feet-first landing the body stops over a distance \(d\) of leg flexion and ground
give, so the mean force for a vertical arrival speed \(v\) is
\begin{equation}
  F = \frac{m v^2}{2 d}, \qquad \text{shared between two legs.}
  \label{eq:impact}
\end{equation}
The tibia breaks under axial and combined loads of \SIrange{\nTibiaLo}{\nTibiaHi}{\kilo\newton}
per leg \autocite{salzar2014leg,abidin2026tibia,forman2012femur,ivarsson2005bending,femurFE2022},
which sets a fracture-onset speed \(v^\star = \sqrt{2 d F_\mathrm{tot}/m}\) for
\(d = \SIrange{10}{35}{\centi\meter}\).
Two arrivals are compared for the cases that reach \SI{200}{\meter}.
In a controlled glide that ends without a flare, the vertical speed is the one at which
\cref{eq:eom} meets the ground.
After a loss of pitch control the craft strikes at airspeed \(V\) along a path
\ang{\nDepAngLo}--\ang{\nDepAngHi} below horizontal, with vertical speed \(V\sin\theta\).
\Cref{eq:impact} uses the mean force, so real peak loads are higher and the onset speeds are
upper bounds.
