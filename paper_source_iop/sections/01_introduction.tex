\section{Introduction}

William of Malmesbury, writing his \textit{Gesta Regum Anglorum} in about 1125, records
that Eilmer, an older monk of his own abbey, had in his youth fixed wings to his hands and
feet, launched from a tower, and travelled ``more than a furlong'' before he fell, broke both
legs, and was lame for the rest of his life \autocite{williamMalmesbury_gesta}.
Eilmer, William adds, used to say that he fell because he had forgotten to fit a tail.
Lynn White's study of the passage established it as the earliest credible account of a
gliding attempt in Western Europe \autocite{white1961eilmer}.

The account is short, but it states a launch point, a distance, a wind, a failure, an injury,
and a cause.
Each of these can be checked against physics that is taught to undergraduates.
This paper asks whether the event William describes is physically possible at Malmesbury,
and whether a missing tail is enough to explain the fall.
It does not ask whether Eilmer understood aerodynamics, and it does not try to reconstruct
his apparatus, which no source describes beyond its wings.
The unknown apparatus is instead treated as a range of plausible parameters and sampled.

The problem makes a compact teaching case for undergraduate mechanics.
It assumes calculus, Newtonian mechanics, and elementary probability, and suits students
from the second year onwards and their instructors.
Answering it needs the energy balance of a glider at launch, flight in a moving air mass,
lift over a slope, the neutral-point criterion for pitch stability, the growth rate of an
unstable mode, Monte Carlo estimation with confidence intervals, and the mechanics of a
feet-first impact.
Each step uses public data and a few lines of code, and each produces a number that can
be compared with the historical text.
\Cref{sec:teaching} sets the steps as exercises.

\Cref{sec:source} summarises the source and the site, \cref{sec:methods} the models,
\cref{sec:results} the results, and \cref{sec:discussion} what they do and do not show.
