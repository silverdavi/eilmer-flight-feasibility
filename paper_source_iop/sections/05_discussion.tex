\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the account requires}

A furlong from a tower of this height needs a strong southwest wind, a wing near the better
end of what cloth and timber allow, and a track that stays over the slope.
The headwind that gives Eilmer his launch speed also holds him back over the ground, so on
the straight line out over the valley that \textcite{woosnam1986eilmer} proposed he runs out
of slope first.
Winds of \SI{\nSWStrong}{\meter\per\second} or more blow about \nSWEightHours\ hours a year, so a man
who waited for one would not wait long, and William's phrase that Eilmer
``collected the breeze'' fits a launch timed to such a wind.

Without a tail the craft diverges in pitch within a fraction of a second, close to human
reaction time.
By shifting his body Eilmer could hold the attitude for a while, so tens of seconds of flight
and then a loss of control fit the model.
William names the trigger, the violence of the wind and the swirling of the air, and a gust
that demanded a faster correction than he could make would end the flight in under a second
(\cref{fig:stability}a).
The models show only that he was unlikely to keep control, and that a tail would have
removed the need.

The broken legs are consistent with a loss of control but do not prove one.
Gliding in without a flare is too slow to break them in this model.
The injury needs a fast, steep arrival, which a loss of pitch control gives, but so would a
stall at height or a dive unrelated to the tail.

\subsection{Which results depend on the assumptions}

Three results rest on measurement or simple bounds, not on the sampled ranges.
The slope faces the prevailing wind, and strong southwest winds are measured
(\cref{fig:site,fig:wind}).
The launch dive costs \((V^2-v_0^2)/2g\) of height whatever the wing, which rules out a
still-air furlong from this tower.
A tailless wing is unstable in pitch whenever the CG is aft of about \num{\nCGonset}\,\(c\),
and a man fixed to it at his hands and feet has his CG near mid-chord.
This would fail only if his mass centre lay ahead of \num{\nCGonset}\,\(c\), near his hands
(\cref{fig:stability}b).

The fraction of cases reaching \SI{\nTarget}{\meter} does depend on the ranges in Supplementary
\cref{tab:priors}, and should be read as the share of the sampled ranges that works, not as a
probability that the flight happened.
It is most sensitive to the glide ratio and the ridge lift.
The smoothed \SI{\nDEMres}{\meter} terrain grid understates the real slope, which would raise the
lift, while turbulence and separation behind the spur edge would lower it.
The point-mass model holds \(C_L\) fixed, so the pilot cannot trade height for speed better
than a stable glider, and the pitch analysis is longitudinal only.
A large-eddy simulation over laser-scanned terrain and a six-degree-of-freedom model with a pilot
would narrow these margins.

\subsection{Limits of the comparison}

The comparison assumes that a furlong means about \SI{\nTarget}{\meter} of ground, that the tower
stood at or near the abbey, and that William reports what Eilmer said.
A shorter flight would be easier to explain, and a tower on lower ground harder.
No model here tests whether the structure could carry the flight loads.
