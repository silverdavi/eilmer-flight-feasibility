\begin{abstract}
\noindent
William of Malmesbury records that in the early eleventh century a monk of his abbey,
Eilmer, fixed wings to his hands and feet, launched from a tower, travelled ``more than a
furlong'' (about \SI{200}{\meter}), fell, broke both legs, and later blamed the fall on
having no tail.
This paper tests whether that account is consistent with elementary flight physics, using
public data and methods at the level of an undergraduate mechanics course: a digital
elevation model of the site, twenty years of reanalysis wind data, a slope-lift estimate, a
point-mass glide integrated from a standing launch, a linear pitch-stability analysis, a
Monte Carlo sample over the unknown apparatus and launch day, and an impact model for the
legs.
A glider leaving the tower at walking pace must lose about \SI{\nHeightToTrim}{\meter} of
height to reach its \SI{\nTrimSpeed}{\meter\per\second} flying speed, so a long flight needs
a strong wind.
Flown out over the valley into that wind, none of \nSamples\ sampled cases covers
\SI{200}{\meter}.
Flown along the southwest-facing slope in ridge lift, \SI{\nReachSlope}{\percent} do
(\SI{95}{\percent} confidence interval \numrange{\nReachSlopeLo}{\nReachSlopeHi}\,\%), with a
median launch wind of \SI{\nMedWindReach}{\meter\per\second}.
Without a tail the wing diverges in pitch, with a median doubling time of
\SI{\nTdoubleMC}{\second}, comparable to human reaction time, during a flight of about
\SI{\nMedFlightReach}{\second}; a tail removes the divergence.
A controlled arrival, even without a flare, does not break the legs; an arrival after loss of
pitch control often does.
The account is physically possible but marginal, and the missing tail is a sufficient cause
of the fall.
Each step is posed as a student exercise, with worked answers.
\end{abstract}
